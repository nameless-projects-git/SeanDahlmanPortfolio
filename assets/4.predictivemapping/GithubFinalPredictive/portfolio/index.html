01 · The data

Three federal datasets, one question

Every Forest Service timber harvest in Idaho since 1900, every invasive weed survey since 1934, and every ranger district boundary in the state, joined at the polygon level and sampled to a clean point dataset.

    Click a district in the hero map to see its enhanced-management rate, top genera, and harvest acreage.

    02 · The model

    Disturbance ecology > district identity

    A Random Forest classifier predicts whether a location requires enhanced post-harvest weed management. Trained on 63,503 Idaho survey records and validated by holding out entire ranger districts (GroupKFold spatial cross-validation, so the model has to generalize to a district it has never seen).

    AUC = 0.70 ± 0.07 5-fold spatial CV, district held out

    Features include genus, land ownership, elevation, distance to nearest harvest, years since that harvest (computed from DATE_COMPL × DATE_COLLE), and distance to the nearest TIGER primary/secondary road.

    What predicts invasion

    Permutation importance, each feature is shuffled and the AUC drop measured directly. The conservative, distribution-free read on what the model actually uses.

    Disturbance proximity (years since harvest, distance to harvest, distance to road) and terrain dominate. Species composition matters but is a secondary signal once disturbance ecology is accounted for.

    Why this matters

    Spatial cross-validation forces the model to generalize across districts, not memorize them. The fold-to-fold variation (std 0.065) reflects real heterogeneity between Idaho's ranger districts, not statistical noise. AUC = 0.70 means the model meaningfully outperforms random (0.50) on locations it has never seen, which is the entire point of a "predictive" map.

    03 · Specialist or enhanced?

    No invasive in Idaho is a pure harvest specialist

    A common framing claims certain genera (Linaria, Chondrilla) only appear after timber disturbance. Checked at the species level statewide:

    Of the 116 invasive species in the dataset, 74 are harvest-enhanced (present both in harvest zones and elsewhere on the landscape), 42 are baseline only, and 0 are pure harvest specialists. The original "specialist" claim only holds at the district × species pair level (14 such pairs out of 1,073).

    The operational reframing: post-harvest treatment isn't fighting unique invaders. It's fighting the same 74 species already on the landscape, with disturbance amplifying their establishment. Pre-harvest planning still matters, but for intensity, not novelty.

    04 · Where they concentrate

    Mid-elevation hotspot, 1,200–2,000 m

    Mid-elevation zones carry the heaviest invasion load, the same band where timber operations are economically viable. Hieracium and Cynoglossum push higher; Centaurea and Euphorbia spread more evenly.

    05 · Limitations

    Honest about what this model can't claim

    1. TIGER primary/secondary roads only. Forest roads, the actual access points for harvest crews, aren't in the public TIGER file. Real distance-to-road would use the FSGeodata Forest Service Roads layer.
    2. Survey effort still skews counts. Districts surveyed more often have more positive points. Weighting by survey effort per district per year would help.
    3. Constructed target. enhanced_mgmt is defined as cover_pct ≥ 1 OR (recent harvest within 500 m AND ≤ 7 years). Defensible but not ground truth.
    4. Anachronistic roads. TIGER roads are from 2024; surveys go back to 1934. The road network from earlier eras is approximated by today's network.
    5. Genus truncation. 91.4% genus match rate; two genera (Cynoglossu, Leucanthem) show up as 10-character DBF-truncated names.

    Data: USDA FSGeodata Clearinghouse, USGS National Map. Code: vanilla HTML + MapLibre GL JS + D3. No backend, no build step.
